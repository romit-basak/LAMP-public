## 2026-08-13 (later) — Interior features made into *targets*; the frescoes are on the dome and out of reach from outside

- **New `scripts/test_feature_visibility.py`.** Niches and apses stop being occluders and become the things looked at. Observers stand 1.5 m outside an opening on its axis at 1.5 m eye height; external observers are other chapels' door stations within 60 m. Of **175 interior features on 79 chapels: 44 (25%) are visible from their own doorway, 26 (15%) from another chapel, 121 (69%) from nowhere tested.** Apses do worse than niches — 2 of 7 from their own door, **0 from any other chapel**.
- **New `opening_rect()` in `aperture_registry.py`, extracted from the builder's inline block.** The sightline test has to agree with the mesh about where an opening is to the millimetre, and a second implementation would have quietly disagreed. Same for `built_thickness()` lifted out of `build_aperture_walls.main`. Both verified: **all 197 frozen meshes still byte-identical.**
- **Recess targets sit *inside* the pocket, at mid-depth**, mirroring the builder's face convention (`in` recedes from the inner face toward the ring, `out` the reverse). Placing them 0.5 m out in the room — what the entrance-axis target does — would make the pocket's own reveal irrelevant and count a niche as seen from angles that only ever saw the wall beside it. 4 targets dropped on zero-thickness walls, where the builder drops the pocket too.
- **The window-frames-a-niche result is a placement artifact and is reported as one.** 8 chapels show a window whose axis lands 4-13 deg off an opposite-wall niche. But **no source anywhere gives an along-wall position**: `source_pos=report` means the report named the *wall*, and every `s_m` comes from the spacing rule in `curate_windows.py` / `curate_niches.py`. Opposite-wall features placed near wall centres align by construction. Nothing finer than wall attribution is claimable.
- **The wall-level version of the same question is real, and the engine independently recovers it.** The report explicitly places a niche in the wall *facing the entrance* for **24 chapels** ("wall facing the entrance", 23 occurrences + 1 with unknown entrance). Among the 20 of those with a modelled niche, **55.6% of niches are visible from the chapel's own doorway, against 16.7% elsewhere** — a 3.3x difference recovered from geometry alone, on wall attributions that are evidenced. This is the door-to-sacred-object configuration, and it is a recurring type at El Bagawat rather than one special chapel.
- **New `scripts/read_report_paintings.py`.** Chapters IV and V describe the two painted chapels scene by scene. **27 named scenes extracted (17 in chapel 30, 10 in chapel 80), with `--crossval` matching the report's own printed running order 17/17** — Fakhry listing his own scenes is independent of the headings the parser finds, so that is real evidence. Positions are recorded in two classes: absolute (compass wall, dome, pendentive, entrance arch) and relative ("next to the last scene"), the latter usable because the scenes are numbered and described in order around the chamber.
- **The frescoes are on the dome, and this is decisive for the visibility question.** Chapter V, p.68: *"I begin here with Adam and Eve which are in the east side of the dome and I continue the description of the scenes anti clockwise."* **New `scripts/test_painting_visibility.py`** measures the consequence rather than asserting it: from the standing station outside chapel 30's door, the highest visible interior point at the chamber centre is **1.41 m above the floor datum against a springing line at 2.49 m — a 1.08 m shortfall**. The door head caps how steeply a sightline can rise once inside, so the entire painted surface is invisible from outside. The bound is deliberately generous (empty chamber, observer free to stand anywhere on the axis, the registry's most favourable head height).
- **Fakhry says the same thing in words**: chapel 80 *"seems to have been always accessible to visitors who came to look at the paintings of the dome and who left to us many graffiti ... which cover the walls from the top to the floor."* The paintings were an entered experience, not a display to the outside.

### Two problems found, both open

- **Chapel 80 — the Chapel of Peace — has no registry row at all**, so no mesh and no station. It is one of the 66 chapels with no door: `read_report_directions.py` parses Chapter VII only, and 80's description is in Chapter V. The single most-studied chapel on the site is missing from the model. Its entrance is recoverable from the same pages already OCR'd.
- **The interior floor datum is per-point, and the terrain under a footprint is not flat.** Every opening sill and target height is derived from the bare DEM sampled at that feature's own position, so within one chapel a niche on the downhill wall sits metres below one on the uphill wall. Measured across 263 footprints: **median spread under the wall midpoints 0.79 m, 33% over 1 m, 9% over 2 m, worst 4.85 m (chapel 258); chapel 30 spans 3.1 m over 15.6 m².** That is comparable to a niche's whole height (0.55 m) and is a live confound in the 69%-seen-from-nowhere figure. Fixing it means a per-chapel floor datum, which changes the meshes and so must land behind a flag with `per-point` kept as the default so the frozen baseline reproduces.

### Mentor-facing note: chapels without a doorway were not sealed

Raised as a hypothesis — that the 66 chapels with no entrance in the data might genuinely have been sealed mausolea. The report rules it out for the chapel itself and confirms it one level down:

- Chapter II: *"There are no doors preserved now but in one chapel only there is a frame of wood left in its place (chapel No. 211). The doors were of wood and the places of their bolts are still preserved in many."* Bolts imply repeated opening; thresholds and brick door jambs are described as general features. No chapel anywhere in the report is described as entrance-less.
- The sealing is the **burial shaft**, cut into the chapel floor and leading to a chamber below — and Fakhry excluded it from the record: *"The burial shafts inside the chapels are completely ignored in my plans; I have not cleaned any one of them and it is beyond my purpose to describe them or even to indicate their places."* So the model is missing a floor-level feature by the source's own choice, with no geometry available for it.
- The no-door set *is* architecturally distinct — smaller (median 16.5 vs 19.8 m²), lower (3 vs 4 m), 63% Type 1 against 38% — but that is exactly the reporting bias expected when the plainest chapels get the shortest entries, and **46 of the 66 are never described in the report at all**, in contiguous ID runs. The two explanations are confounded; the evidence above favours the reporting gap.

## 2026-08-12 (later) — Entrance orientation is not solar, and not topographic either; visual targets built; Phase 3 extractor landed

- New **`scripts/test_entrance_azimuth.py`**. No meshes, no rays, runs in seconds — the cheap test that constrains the explanation space before any expensive run. **All three nulls rejected.**
- **The solar hypothesis fails in the strongest possible way.** At Kharga (phi 25.44) the sunrise arc is 63.9-116.1 deg and the sunset arc 243.9-296.1 deg. Both give **probability zero to south**, which is the single largest class at 74 of 194. A null that forbids the modal class is falsified outright, so the script reports that instead of manufacturing a finite G from a pseudocount.
- **Uniform is rejected too** (G=366.6, df=7, p=3.4e-75): orientation is neither solar nor arbitrary. Observed S 74 / W 66 / E 52 / SW 2, and **zero chapels in the entire northern half** (N/NE/NW), which is a hard `check()` in the script.
- **The mixture number needed guarding and got it.** A best-fit solar/uniform mixture puts weight **0.452** on the solar component — quoted alone that reads as support for a solar story. It is not: the fitted mixture is itself rejected (G=281.6, df=6, p=7.0e-58), and it predicts ~13 chapels opening north where the truth is zero. The weight is high only because the solar arcs cover E and W, which are common here for other reasons. The script now G-tests the mixture rather than reporting its weight.
- **Topography does not rescue it either.** Entrances sit a median 71.8 deg from straight downhill; 32.5% fall within 45 deg of downhill against **30.2% under permutation** (p=0.261). The chance baseline is permuted rather than asserted — with 45 deg direction bins against a continuous aspect, "what chance gives" is not a number you can eyeball.
- Statistical choice worth defending to mentors: a **multinomial G-test on 45 deg bins**, not a Rayleigh/V-test. The report states directions in words, so the data are 8 compass classes; a Rayleigh test on four spikes at 90 deg spacing returns a large resultant that reflects the binning, not the architecture.
- New **`scripts/build_visual_targets.py`** → `target_inventory.csv` + `visual_targets.gpkg` (PointZ, per-kind layers, EPSG:32636, inspectable in QGIS before any visibility run). **256 centroid + 192 entrance_axis targets.** Target z is derived from the registry (bare ground + opening mid-height, `z_source` recorded) and **never sampled from a heightfield** — that is the exact trap that made the raster door-effect meaningless in August.
- **Seven ruined chapels dropped, not clamped** (IDs 21/39/83/99/203/209/220, `Elevation` 0.7-1.8 m). Their eye-level targets sit at or above the surviving roof plane, where a target is visible from everywhere; counting one as "seen" would measure the ruin, not the architecture. The headroom filter is a flag with a stated threshold rather than a silent clamp.
- New **`scripts/read_report_features.py`** (Phase 3), importing `read_report_directions`'s OCR/paging/entry-splitting rather than copying them. Candidate yields, after dedupe: **niches 131 chapels (80 wall-anchored), light apertures 52 (34), apses 15 (5), pillars 48 (0 by design)**. Wall-anchoring is up from the earlier feasibility audit's 53 niche / 30 aperture chapels — the conjunction grammar is what buys that.

## 2026-08-12 — "How far off-axis can you still see inside?", answered by measurement; new `aperture_envelope.py`

- New **`scripts/aperture_envelope.py`**: stands an observer on a (range x off-normal angle) grid outside a chapel's door and reports what fraction of the interior floor is visible, ray-cast in the real scene with the neighbours meshed. Writes a CSV, a figure and headline angles. `--mesh-dir` picks the wall fabric, so legacy and evidence-based thickness go side by side on the same chapel.
- **The engine lands exactly on the analytic bound, which is a real validation.** Widest ray actually admitted vs `atan(w/t)`: **64.7 vs 65.1 deg** at 0.40 m, **71.6 vs 72.0 deg** at 0.28 m. Two independent things — a closed-form aperture condition and a triangle-mesh ray caster — agreeing to under half a degree.
- **Answer for chapel 210 (door 0.86 m, CAD-measured wall 0.28 m):** on axis you see 34-68% of the floor; **half the floor needs +/-38 deg, a quarter +/-52 deg, and everything is gone past ~70 deg.**
- **Distance costs area, not access.** On axis the visible fraction falls 68% at 1 m to 37% at 8 m and then flattens — 34% at 20 m. It asymptotes because the reachable interior is the doorway projected from the eye, which tends to the door's own width rather than to zero. So *any* interior point stays visible from right across the necropolis; what shrinks is how much of the room is in the wedge. Worth stating plainly to mentors, because it means door-mediated visibility is a long-range effect, not a doorstep one.
- **Thickness shows up exactly where the mechanism predicts — in the oblique tail, not head-on.** With the interior floor pinned to a common inset so both runs share a denominator, on-axis visibility is unchanged (42.2% vs 43.5% at 4 m) while the cutoff moves from **62-68 deg to 70-72 deg**, and angles where the legacy wall sees nothing (60 deg, 68 deg) become 1.4% visible. That is the door-reveal effect isolated, and it is the reason thickness mattered enough to measure.
- **Two bugs found in this script and fixed before any number was believed.** (1) The registry's `s_m` is the opening's *centre* (the builder cuts `s-w/2, s+w/2`), but the first version offset by another half width — so the "on-axis" observer stood half a doorway off axis, which inflated the half-floor angle from +/-38 to +/-70 deg. (2) Insetting the interior samples by each run's own thickness gave the two fabrics *different floors*, so their percentages shared no denominator; `--target-inset` now pins it. A render of the on-axis station was what exposed the first one — the doorway sat visibly off-centre in frame.
- Renders at four stations (3 m and 8 m on axis, 4 m at 45 and 70 deg) for both fabrics, natural + depth: `250_Apertures/observer_views_meshes{,_fabric}/`. At 70 deg the doorway is a hairline slit in both, which is the envelope's cutoff seen directly.

## 2026-08-11 (later) — Investigated why the doorless graph baseline is nonzero at all (13/1,052 pairs "centroid-visible" with zero doors anywhere); resolved, and it strengthens confidence in the +23% door-effect number

- User's question: if doors are the only way to see inside a solid-walled building, why does the *doorless* control show any centroid-visible pairs at all, before doors are even added back? A fair challenge — a nonzero baseline needed its own explanation before the +23% jump on top of it means anything.
- Ruled out first: `footprints.geometry.centroid` landing outside the true polygon (a classic GIS gotcha for concave shapes) — checked all 197 registered buildings, zero fall outside either the true footprint or the canonical mesh wall ring.
- **Reproduced the phenomenon directly**: built all 197 buildings as solid (doorless) meshes and reran the 3-observer/radius-60 graph. Got 9/789 pairs "centroid-visible" — same phenomenon, smaller absolute numbers (3 vs. the original run's 4 observers).
- **Full explanation, verified**: every one of the 9 flagged buildings has **no registry row and no mesh file** — they are plain, unmeshed heightfield blocks, not meshed buildings with solid-but-doorless walls. For an unmeshed building, `surface_z` at the centroid returns the ORIGINAL block's roof height (confirmed to the cm for one case: bare ground 106.78 m + this building's 3 m elevation field = 109.78 m, matching the graph's reported target height of 109.772 m). So "centroid visible" here just means "this nearby building's own rooftop is visible" — ordinary skyline visibility, unrelated to doors, walls, or apertures. A door could never have changed this pair either way, since there's no wall there to put one in.
- **This is good news, not a new bug**: it confirms the doorless-vs-apertured graph comparison (the one behind the +23% figure) is not confounded the way the raster metric was. For unmeshed buildings, doorless and apertured are identical (same block, same target height) → correctly zero effect. For meshed buildings, both variants share identical wall/roof geometry and the same flattened-floor target basis — the only thing that can differ is the door holes. So the graph's aperture-effect number was never mixing "wrong target height" into the door signal the way `compare_apertures.py`'s raster sweep was; it just also carries this unrelated "some buildings have no mesh yet" baseline noise, which is now identified and explained rather than left as an unexplained wrinkle.

## 2026-08-11 — Correction: "too small a sample" was the wrong explanation for the zero door effect; it's a structural blind spot in this raster sweep, not a sample-size problem

- User noticed the red/blue patches had nearly vanished after the ground-cells fix below and asked whether that means apertures barely matter, or the sample is just weak. Good question to press on — the entry below's explanation ("this ROI is too small a sample") turns out to be wrong, not just incomplete.
- **Checked where the surviving doorless-vs-apertured flips land**: on the (confounded) all-cells metric, the 6-8 flipped cells per condition sit **100% inside building footprints** — i.e. exactly the cells the ground-only fix excludes. That's the tell.
- **Root cause: `target_grid` fixes every target cell's height once, from the ORIGINAL with-buildings DEM, before any scene variant is built.** A cell inside a footprint is therefore always tested at the block's **roof** height — in `bare`, `doorless`, and `apertured` alike. A door sits near ground level, well below that test height, so it can essentially never change whether that point is visible. More observers or more buildings would not fix this — it's not a statistics problem, the raster sweep is asking the wrong question at those cells regardless of sample size.
- **The visibility graph's centroid test does not have this problem.** `build_viewgraph`'s per-building test calls `scene.surface_z()` on the actual scene in play; for a meshed building that goes through `HybridScene.surface_z` → `__getattr__` → the flattened base scene, i.e. the true interior floor height, not the roof (the code comment there, `# extruded roof`, is stale — written when buildings were solid blocks only). That's why the site-wide graph run already found a real, positive door effect (+23% centroid-visible pairs) while this raster script structurally cannot.
- **Corrected the report's door-effect paragraph** (`compare_apertures.py`) to state the actual mechanism instead of "too small a sample," and reran both resolutions — numbers unchanged, wording fixed.
- Net answer to "do apertures matter": yes, on the instrument built to measure it (the visibility graph); this particular raster comparison was never capable of showing it, independent of resolution or sample size.

## 2026-08-10 (later still) — The comparison was not apples-to-apples: the "wall/roof effect" was mostly a rooftop-target artifact. Metric corrected to ground cells

- User asked whether the comparison is really like-for-like. It was not, and the reason turned out to be **different from the one I first proposed** — worth recording, because the first diagnosis would have cost a lot of work for nothing.
- **The proposed fix was wrong.** I had suggested replacing the mesh's least-squares roof plane with per-cell terrain-following sampling, to make `doorless` match `bare`. Checking the code first: `plane_fit` **already** fits through `ground(ring vertex) + Elevation`, so "sample the DEM at roof vertices" is a no-op, and matching the heightfield's interior would have required tessellating every roof cap at DEM resolution (~+63k triangles site-wide). Then measurement killed the premise outright: the mesh roof is on average **0.068 m *lower*** than the heightfield roof (lower on 46% of cells vs higher on 26%) — the **wrong sign** to explain the mesh occluding *more*. Plan-area was also the wrong sign (mesh 2.9% *smaller* than the DEM's raised cells). Roof modelling was not the cause.
- **The actual cause: rooftop targets.** `target_grid` pins every target point to the **heightfield** surface, so a cell inside a footprint gets its target at the extruded block's roof height — and each mesh variant is then asked whether that same fixed point is visible against its own, slightly different, roof. The mismatch is asymmetric: a target that lands inside the mesh is definitively blocked, while one floating just above it is only *conditionally* visible. So it biases every mesh variant against the heightfield **regardless of apertures**. Measured: **77-92% of all bare-vs-doorless flips fall inside building footprints**, not in shadowed ground behind them.
- **Fix: ground cells are now the primary metric** (footprint cells excluded, `all_touched`). Effect on the 0.4 m ROI: `wall_roof_effect` **-392 → -63 cells** (84% of it was the artifact). The all-cells numbers are still written to the report, explicitly labelled confounded, so the two can be contrasted. Figures and the bar chart are ground-only now too; the agreement panels went from large red rooftop patches to almost entirely green.
- **This also retracts the door-effect claim from the entry below.** The +6/+8 cells I reported as "the door effect resolving at 0.4 m" were **rooftop cells** — on ground cells the door effect here is **0**. Doors cannot plausibly reveal a rooftop at block height, so those cells were the artifact, not evidence. Corrected in the report. The real aperture evidence remains the site-wide 0.4 m run (2026-08-08): **+115 ground cells / +0.28%** over 197 buildings and 4 observers, and **+23% centroid-visible pairs** on the visibility graph — that run measured ground cells and a far larger sample. This 74-building/3-observer ROI is simply too small to register it.
- **The residual -63 is a discretization artifact, and it behaves like one**: it is the heightfield's bilinearly-ramped building edges vs the mesh's true vertical wall on the exact footprint boundary — two representations of the same polygon that don't produce identical silhouettes. As a share of visible ground it is **13.7% at 1.5 m and 0.5% at 0.4 m**, shrinking ~27x as the cell shrinks. A real geometric disagreement would not scale that way; a silhouette-quantization term does.
- **Dome effect on ground visibility is exactly zero** in every condition (it read -17 to -79 on the all-cells metric). Consistent, not suspicious: a dome sits above roof level, so it can only occlude ground reached by grazing over a rooftop, and at 1.5-1.75 m eye height in this tight ROI that ground is already blocked by the wall beneath.
- Both resolutions rerun on the corrected metric; `compare_baseline.py` untouched and still reproducing its validated numbers.

## 2026-07-13 — Volume mesh output: the viewshed's 3D *shape* as a surface (`--volume-format mesh`)

- User asked for a volume output that stores the 3D shape (surfaces/edges) instead of individual voxels. New shared module **`scripts/volume_mesh.py`** (deliberately torch-free so `volume_convert.py` keeps its light startup) + wiring: `viewshed.py --volume-format mesh` with `--mesh-style {blocky,smooth}` (per-observer and combined-union meshes, `viewshed_volume_{label}_mesh.ply` + 3D QC PNG) and `volume_convert.py --to mesh` (from a saved CSV; documented staircase caveat — its grid re-bins absolute z, the engine path is the faithful shape).
- Design: extraction runs in index space; a single `index_to_world` choke point applies the **terrain-following warp** (volume levels are heights above local ground, so vertex z = bilinear ground + level; NaN ground nearest-filled first) and the orientation flip.
